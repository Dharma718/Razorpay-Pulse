Detect → Investigate → Quantify → Recommend → Act → Learn

Every stage is a discrete, inspectable step. The LLM only narrates — it never decides the diagnosis or the action.

Guardrails

Autonomy is earned, not assumed

Confidence alone never authorizes an action — risk tier matters just as much.

01

Low confidence → investigate further

Below 60% confidence, Pulse never guesses. It flags the pattern for manual analyst review instead of picking an action.

02

High confidence + low risk → auto

Only when both confidence is high AND the action type is inherently low-risk (like a customer notice) does Pulse act without waiting for a human.

03

Medium/high risk → approval required

Even at 90%+ confidence, actions like re-routing payment method recommendations wait for a human click before executing.

04

Merchant-side patterns → human only, always

If every payment method degrades at once, Pulse recognizes this likely isn't a bank issue at all — and refuses to automate a fix regardless of confidence.

About This Project

Going beyond "something's wrong" alerts

Razorpay's own RAY Concierge already monitors critical metrics and alerts merchants before failures escalate. Razorpay's Agent Studio already ships production agents for cart recovery, disputes, subscriptions, cashflow forecasting, and settlement insights.

Pulse is deliberately positioned in the gap those don't cover: when the top-line success rate metric looks only mildly off, but a specific bank, processor, or region is quietly failing far worse underneath it. Pulse investigates that gap with evidence, quantifies what it's actually costing, and only acts within limits a human has set.

Design Decisions & Rationale

Why it's built this way, not some other way

Every non-obvious choice below was deliberate — here's the reasoning, so it doesn't have to be taken on faith.

Why does this require signing in?

Because a real fintech dashboard never would let anyone see payment data, revenue figures, or incident actions without authentication — even though the data here is synthetic, gating it practices the access-control habit a production system actually needs, rather than skipping it because "it's just a demo."

Why is there a Demo Account button?

So anyone evaluating this project can get into the live, fully interactive product in one click — full read/write access, no account creation, no waiting on credentials.

Why does the Demo Account have full read/write access, including clearing history?

So an evaluator can genuinely try every feature exactly as an owner would, with nothing held back. The real distinction between accounts is session length — a demo session expires after 2 hours, an owner session lasts 7 days — rather than which buttons are visible.

Why can individual history entries be deleted one at a time, not just cleared all at once?

Bulk-clearing is a blunt instrument. If one entry is wrong, outdated, or just noise from testing, removing just that one preserves everything else worth keeping as precedent for future incidents.

Why is so much of the data simulated?

There's no access to real Razorpay production transactions. A realistic traffic simulator generates the data instead, and every simulated figure is labeled clearly as such — never presented as if it were real.

Why does Gemini only explain, never decide?

So an LLM's mistake — a hallucination, a misread sentence — can never directly cause a payment action on its own. The category, the confidence score, and the resulting action are deterministic, testable code. Gemini narrates what already happened; it doesn't get a vote in what happens next.

Why do some incidents need human approval, and others get fully blocked from automation?

Confidence alone isn't a safe basis for autonomy — risk matters just as much. A pattern that looks like a merchant's own broken checkout should never be "fixed" automatically by an AI guessing at infrastructure changes, no matter how confident the diagnosis is.

Why does a resolved incident stay on screen until manually dismissed?

So the person reviewing it decides when they're done looking, not a timer. Losing context because a card silently vanished mid-review is worse than one extra click.

Why is there a free-form "describe your own issue" option alongside the fixed scenario buttons?

Real problems don't always match a preset list. Typing, speaking, or screenshotting an actual issue and running it through the exact same evidence-based, guardrailed pipeline shows the system generalizes past canned demo buttons — not just click-throughs.

Why are revenue figures always "estimated" or "recoverable," never "revenue saved"?

Because nothing was actually saved in a simulation. Overstating results is the fastest way to lose credibility with anyone technical reviewing this — so the language stays honest even when it's less flattering.

Why does the system remember past incidents?

To demonstrate outcome-based learning instead of a stateless one-off tool — the next similar incident can cite exactly what happened last time, the way an experienced analyst would recall precedent rather than starting from zero every time.

Why one single dashboard instead of separate merchant/analyst/admin views?

Three interfaces would spend limited time on UI plumbing instead of the actual hard problem — detecting, investigating, and safely acting on a payment incident. One focused, capable dashboard demonstrates more engineering judgment than three thin ones, and it's what a real evaluator actually wants to see in a short review.
